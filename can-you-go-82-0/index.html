Can an NBA team really go 82–0?

No NBA team has ever finished a season 82–0. The best regular season on record is the 2015–16 Golden State Warriors' 73–9, which broke the 72–10 mark the 1995–96 Chicago Bulls had held for twenty years. TRUE 82 exists because that question is more fun as a draft challenge than as a trivia answer.

Why is 82–0 close to impossible?

Going undefeated is close to impossible because a team would need roughly a 99.16% chance of winning every single game just to have a coin-flip (50%) shot at a perfect season. In a modern, high-scoring environment, that works out to about a +38.8 net rating, a level no team in NBA history has come near.

That figure comes from Daryl Morey's Pythagorean win model:

Win% = PF13.91 ÷ (PF13.91 + PA13.91)

PF = points scored, PA = points allowed.

And +38.8 only buys a 50% chance. Real seasons pile on the variance you cannot model away: cross-country travel, back-to-backs, injuries, cold shooting nights, getting jobbed by refs, and 29 opponents who treat beating you as their season highlight. All of it has to be absorbed by an outperformance cushion so large the arithmetic essentially rules perfection out. Morey's model may bend at the extremes, but it is built to describe what is actually possible, and 82–0 is not.

So if TRUE 82 ever feels too hard, know that it is going easy on you: the required net rating drops to +27.1, and your starters get to play a full 48 minutes for all 82 games (Joel Embiid breathes a sigh of relief).

Who came closest to going undefeated in the NBA?

Nobody has come close. The three most dominant regular seasons ever still finished a long way from perfect:

2015–16 Golden State Warriors73–9+10.7+25.4
1995–96 Chicago Bulls72–10+13.4+23.1
1971–72 Los Angeles Lakers69–13+10.7+23.4

"Short of 82–0" is the net rating each team would still need to add to reach a coin-flip shot at a perfect season in its own scoring era, which is why two teams at +10.7 sit different distances from the line.

Even those runs came in bursts, not full seasons: the Warriors opened 2015–16 at 24–0, and the Lakers won 33 straight in 1971–72, still the longest winning streak in NBA history.

So the next time you are mad about a run that stops short of 82–0, the system is being fair to you. Probably cartoonishly generous, actually.

How TRUE 82 turns the question into a game

Real rosters are stuck with one era, one payroll, and one front office. TRUE 82 removes those limits and adds its own: draft five players from random franchise-and-era tickets (two guards, two forwards, one center), and a BPM-based season engine projects whether that lineup could run the table. The engine cares about roster fit, so the question stops being "can you name five legends" and becomes "can you build a team."

The engine also compresses superstar value on purpose, for the same reason eight players who each average 20 points a game will not combine for 160: there is only one ball to go around (cough, Matt Ishbia and Vivek). And it punishes weak defense harder than you would expect. On a roster full of legendary playmakers, elite offense does not make up for being an easy target on the other end the way it might on an ordinary team.

What kind of roster actually has a chance?

The kind that does everything well, not just a stack of pantheon names. A real contender has to:

  1. Defend. Real team defense is what stabilizes the game-to-game variance that ends undefeated runs.
  2. Space the floor. That means at least three players respected from the 3-point line (or who would have been, in eras before it existed). You can sometimes get away with two shooters, but it almost never works.
  3. Carry a super-role player. On a team of legends, someone has to contribute without touches. Ask Chris Bosh, or really ask Mario Chalmers, the #5 option on the Heatles.
  4. Fill every box-score column. The engine runs on BPM, so it wants to see all the counting stats. When 2024–25 Kevin Durant grades out like the team's Mario Chalmers, it is because he was below average at everything except efficient scoring.

So the goal is not to rebuild a Team USA All-Star roster. It is to cover every weakness, at least as far as BPM can detect it.
